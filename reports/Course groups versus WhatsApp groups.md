# Build course groups inside the CILAS app

**Recommendation: build a small groups feature inside the current app, and test it with a pilot of one term.** Do not try to replace WhatsApp for quick talk. Use the app for the work that WhatsApp cannot do: structured discussion, a record, and privacy. The research shows that discussion helps learning only when a teacher designs and guides it. Weekly prompts, roles and synthesis posts have the best evidence. An open chat stream has the weakest. The social side of learning follows the same rule. Groups help learning when people do structured course work together and can see who the others are. In one MOOC experiment, grouping and introductions alone did not raise completion. Structured collaboration gives knowledge gains of about g = 0.42, and belonging gives small but real gains where the setting offers real chances to connect. WhatsApp is good at relationships and quick help. It wins because people already use it. It loses on threads, on a record for the institution, and on privacy, because members can see each other's phone numbers. CILAS already has the parts that make a native build cheap: sessions, enrollments, programme memberships, roles, an editorial review flow, visibility tiers, notifications and Resend email. Discourse is the only external platform that is a good match. But it adds a second large system for one developer to maintain. It also cannot link threads to sessions or send a thread into the article review flow without extra code. The main risk is adoption, not the build. Members will ignore the feature if fellows do not use it every week, or if the alerts do not reach their phones. The pilot must therefore test fellow practice and notifications, not only the software.

## Discussion helps learning only when a fellow designs and guides it

The best evidence comes from the Community of Inquiry (CoI) model. A meta-analysis of 19 studies found that **teaching presence correlates with actual learning at r = .353 and with satisfaction at r = .510** ([Martin et al. 2022](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2604)). Teaching presence has three parts: course design, facilitation and direct instruction. **Cognitive presence correlates with perceived learning at r = .663**, the strongest link in that study. These figures are correlations. They do not prove that discussion causes learning. One quasi-experiment did test cause. With 82 students, **role assignment and regulation scaffolds both raised the level of cognitive presence**, and the two worked better together ([Gašević et al. 2015](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Externally-facilitated-regulation-scaffolding-and-role-assignment/99900601054601842)). A different study found that roles alone gave no improvement ([GMU ITLCP](https://journals.gmu.edu/ITLCP/article/view/640)). Thus structure helps, but no single feature is enough.

Discussions also stop too early. A review found that most student posts stay in the "exploration" and "integration" phases, and **few posts reach "resolution"** ([Regis](https://epublications.regis.edu/facultypubs/745)). A synthesis post, written by the fellow or by a student, is a direct design answer to this known weakness. A qualitative review also says that critical thinking online needs a **multi-step, deliberately facilitated design** ([DiPasquale & Hunter 2018](https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/cjlt/index.php/cjlt/article/view/27782)). The research gives no support for the idea that a forum helps learning just because it exists.

More posts from the teacher are not always better. One older study linked frequent instructor replies to **shorter discussions and fewer student posts** ([Mazzolini & Maddison, cited in Ntourmas et al.](https://www.gsic.uva.es/uploaded_files/44943_Ntourmas_etal_PCI2018_cameraReady_20181123.pdf)). In MOOCs, **starting threads helped participation more than replying**, and this advantage grew with class size ([Du, Wang & Wang 2022](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/7889ab2c-73fb-4a6d-a85c-3ea39fc094c9)). Posting deadlines also increase contributions ([Hew, Cheung & Ng 2010](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/194283)). This supports a "fellow-started weekly prompt" with a deadline, not a fellow who answers every message.

Most members will read and not write. In four moderated networks with 63,990 people, **fewer than 25% ever posted, and superusers made 74.7% of the content** ([van Mierlo 2014](https://jmir.org/2014/2/e33)). The design must therefore serve readers: good summaries, a searchable archive and digests. It must also find and support the few active members, for example alumni or student stewards in the programme and community groups.

Text-anchored discussion has the strongest results for a reading-heavy curriculum. In the Harvard Perusall study, **about 90–95% of students completed nearly all readings, and exam scores were higher by effect sizes of 0.31–0.34** ([Miller et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2018.00008/full)). The authors founded Perusall, and the study did not use random assignment. An independent programming course saw **annotation completion fall from 44% to 21% after one unit** ([arXiv 2407.10322](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.10322)). Social annotation is therefore promising but not proven. It is a good later phase, not the first one.

Knowledge-building research gives the strongest theoretical match to "knowledge made together". On Knowledge Forum, **collaborative inquiry predicted understanding beyond prior achievement**, and one class kept its gains into public exams ([Springer ijCSCL](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11412-009-9069-5)). This theory treats ideas as public objects that a community improves over time. A WhatsApp chat cannot do this, because it has no shared, searchable, linkable record. Most of this evidence comes from schools, not universities.

Two more findings set limits. Anonymity is a trade-off. In one CS1 forum, **women posted anonymously more often, which hid their presence from peers** and weakened their sense of belonging ([OLC abstract](https://conference.onlinelearningconsortium.org/node/12342)). Anonymity also makes students more willing to post on sensitive topics ([St Thomas](https://researchonline.stthomas.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Young-Womens-Sex-Talk-Online-Roles/991015131891803691)). Thus identified posting must be the default, with "anonymous to classmates, visible to the fellow" as an option for questions. Learning communities also give only modest gains. The largest randomised trial, with about 7,000 students, found **about +0.5 credits and no effect on persistence** ([MDRC](https://www.mdrc.org/work/projects/learning-communities-demonstration)). A groups feature alone will not change retention. CILAS must not promise that it will.

## People learn with people, but only a shared task turns contact into learning

The main finding of this section is simple. **Groups help learning when people do structured course work together and can see who the others are.** Contact alone is not enough. In one MOOC experiment, researchers put learners into groups that they chose and introduced them to each other. **This did not raise performance or completion** ([Mercy GER review](https://ger.mercy.edu/index.php/ger/article/view/124/138)). A "join the group" button does not create a learning community. A task, a fellow and visible people do.

### Five theories predict that peers matter, by different paths

Each classic theory names a different social path to learning. For Vygotsky, learning happens first between people and then inside the learner. A learner can do more with help from a "more capable peer" than alone ([Vygotsky 1978](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674576292)). For Lave and Wenger, learning is participation in a community of practice. A newcomer starts with **"legitimate peripheral participation"**: real but low-risk tasks at the edge. The newcomer then moves toward the centre ([Lave & Wenger 1991](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355)). Bandura's social learning theory predicts that a learner gains confidence when they see a peer like themselves work through difficulty. Self-Determination Theory names relatedness as one of three basic needs that feed motivation ([Deci & Ryan 2000](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327965PLI1104_01)). Recent work says that **relatedness is the hardest of the three needs to support online** ([IATED 2026](https://library.iated.org/view/TADDEO2026REL)). Tinto says that students persist when they are integrated academically and socially into the institution ([Tinto 1975](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543045001089)).

Tinto's model has a weak point that matters for CILAS. Tests found **"only modest support for either social or academic integration on community college or commuter campuses"** ([Braxton et al., MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/553666/pdf)). One secondary source reports that **none of 13 testable propositions held for commuter institutions**. Those students have work and family duties and fewer campus friendships ([Braxton et al. 2014, via WIT](https://repository.wit.ie/7812/1/TD_IETI.pdf)). Many CILAS members are adults who study part-time and do not live on a campus. For them, social contact for its own sake is a weak lever. Academic contact with peers about the readings and the work is the stronger lever. This is why course work must be at the centre of the groups, with general community chat at the edge.

### Belonging is real but modest, and it depends on the setting

A 2025 meta-analysis found that a university sense of belonging **correlates with academic outcomes at r ≈ 0.20 and with well-being at r ≈ 0.40** ([Springer 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13384-025-00822-8)). The studies varied widely, and many had a high risk of bias. A scoping review of 69 reports found associations with GPA and persistence that were **mostly positive but small** ([Fong et al., WUSTL](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/a-scoping-review-of-the-associations-between-sense-of-belonging-a/)).

The causal evidence comes from short belonging exercises. In Walton and Cohen's trial with 92 students, a one-hour exercise told students that early difficulty is common and temporary. It **raised Black students' GPA over three years and halved the minority achievement gap**. It had no effect on white students ([Walton & Cohen 2011](https://www.ovid.com/journals/scie/pdf/00007529-201103180-00038~a-brief-social-belonging-intervention-improves-academic-and)). A much larger trial with **26,911 students at 22 institutions** used an online exercise of under 30 minutes. It raised first-year completion by **about 2 percentage points**, mostly for groups that historically persist less ([Walton et al. 2023](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/where-and-with-whom-does-a-brief-social-belonging-intervention-pr/)). The key detail is a condition. The effect appeared **"only when students' groups were afforded opportunities to belong"**. A message of belonging works only when the setting then gives real chances to connect. A groups feature can be that setting. An empty group can send the opposite message: "nobody is here".

### Social presence predicts satisfaction; structured collaboration predicts learning

Social presence is the ability to show yourself as a real person in a mediated space. A meta-analysis of 25 studies found a **strong positive relationship of social presence with satisfaction and with perceived learning** in fully online courses ([Richardson et al. 2017, via ETHE](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-022-00355-5/tables/1)). A 2025 meta-analysis gave a smaller, **moderate** effect ([IJEDE 2025](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ijede/2025-v40-n1-ijede010122/1118768ar)). Both use self-reported outcomes. Social presence helps people stay and come back. That matters for non-degree adults. But it does not prove higher grades.

The evidence for learning gains comes from structured collaboration. A review of 425 studies found that collaboration in computer settings gave **knowledge gains of g = 0.42 and skill gains of g = 0.64**. Scripts and scaffolds added **g = 0.38** for knowledge ([Chen et al. 2018](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/the-role-of-collaboration-computer-use-learning-environments-and-/)). In undergraduate science, small-group learning gave effects of **about 0.51 on achievement and 0.46 on persistence** ([Springer, Stanne & Donovan 1999](https://www2.math.upenn.edu/%7Epemantle/active-papers/springer-stanne-donovan.pdf)). These gains come from designed tasks with a goal and shared work. A free group chat does not meet these conditions.

### Isolation drives dropout claims, and weak ties drive later opportunity

Isolation is often named as a main cause of online dropout, but the claim is **often asserted rather than tested** ([IRRODL 2012](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/1171/2206?inline=1)). A Canadian review of 296 studies found that **belonging, self-efficacy and regular contact with instructors and peers** reduce a distance learner's isolation ([SSHRC](https://www.sshrc-crsh.gc.ca/society-societe/community-communite/ifca-iac/evidence_briefs-donnees_probantes/ksg_evidence_briefs/papi-eng.aspx)). In MOOCs, learners with more social contact complete more often. Many of them study with people they already know ([Cisel 2018](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/3459)). This is a correlation, and self-selection explains part of it. CILAS cohorts are small and have a named fellow, so real ties are more likely than in a MOOC.

Course groups build strong ties. Programme, community and alumni groups build weak ties. The largest network experiment, with **over 20 million LinkedIn users**, found that **moderately weak ties produced the most job mobility**. Strong ties helped more in less digital industries ([Rajkumar et al. 2022](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/2022-rajkumar.pdf)). That study ran without explicit consent and drew criticism ([ACS](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2022/secret-study-conducted-on-20-million-linkedin-users.html)). For CILAS, the lesson is about structure. When a course ends, archive its group, but keep its members in the programme and community groups. This keeps weak ties across cohorts alive. No study of alumni networks in non-degree liberal-arts programmes was found.

### The social layer has costs, and MENA norms shape them

Social layers can bring overload, an "always on" teacher, and exposure. In WhatsApp class groups, teachers reported **irrelevant messages and an expectation of 24-hour availability** ([Bouhnik & Deshen 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)). High-school students **resisted teachers' demands on WhatsApp more than in class** ([Asterhan et al.](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/whatsapp-teacher-student-perspectives-on-teacher-student-whatsapp-2/)). Public post counts and leaderboards invite social comparison. A few confident voices can also dominate a thread. The notes found no direct study of social comparison or dominance in course groups. These risks therefore come from design logic and the participation-inequality data, not from a measured effect.

Arab-context studies point in two directions. Gulf students in US distance programmes described **social shame online, language difficulty and avoidance of confrontation** ([Al-Harthi 2005](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/263)). That study had only six students. A study of 116 Tunisian students found that culture shaped how they took part in online discussion. The authors suggest that **online text spaces can help women who face social pressure** in Arab settings ([Tlili et al. 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jsusta/v13y2021i22p12426-d676202.html)). In Saudi Arabia, online forums allowed cross-gender talk that face-to-face norms limit ([SAGE Convergence 2014](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1354856514534574)). Thus the design question is not "social or not". It is a question of norms, visibility and boundaries. Members need control of their photo and name. Fellows must model polite disagreement, which lowers the threat to face. Polls and reactions give a low-risk first step. Small private task groups give a safer space than a large public thread.

### Informal chat builds relationships; structured threads build knowledge

WhatsApp and the in-app groups do different social jobs. In Bouhnik and Deshen's study, teachers used one WhatsApp group both to **"nurture the social atmosphere"** and as a learning platform. Mixing both jobs in one stream causes the noise problem ([Bouhnik & Deshen 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)). WhatsApp is good for relationships, quick help and logistics. In Saudi Arabia it is **"greatly preferred"** for study support ([SAI](https://ftp.saiconference.com/Downloads/Volume10No8/Paper_53-WhatsApp_as_an_Educational_Support_Tool.pdf)). In Jordan, a teacher-run WhatsApp vocabulary group beat a control group on a post-test ([TPLS](https://tpls.academypublication.com/index.php/tpls/article/view/8889)). Structured, task-shaped threads are where the collaboration gains of g ≈ 0.4–0.6 come from. **No head-to-head study proves this split.** It is a hypothesis that follows from the theory and from the CSCL evidence on structure. CILAS can test it in the pilot.

The design consequence is two spaces inside one app. One space is a light social layer: introductions, reactions, polls and logistics. The other is threaded, task-shaped discussion for course work. This keeps the relationship benefit of a chat, but it does not bury the course content. It also keeps people visible by name and face, which a forum with only text and usernames does not do well.

| Social design move | Evidence behind it | Phase |
|---|---|---|
| Bilingual introduction thread, opened by the fellow in week 0 ("What question brings you here?") | Social presence; belonging needs real chances to connect | 1 |
| Profiles with optional photo and name form the member controls | Social presence; MENA norms on exposure | 1 |
| Visible fellow presence: prompts, modelling of tentative thinking and polite disagreement | Teaching presence; face-saving norms | 1 |
| Polls and reactions as low-risk first steps | Legitimate peripheral participation | 1 |
| Small task sub-groups of 3–5 members | Structured collaboration g ≈ 0.4–0.6; MOOC grouping alone failed | 1 (fellow-made) |
| Quiet hours, digest instead of constant push, no on-call fellows | WhatsApp overload and 24-hour expectation | 1 |
| Hidden phone numbers; CILAS profiles only | Privacy; boundary collapse on WhatsApp | 1 |
| Alumni "how I got through" stories at onboarding | Belonging interventions (Walton & Cohen; Walton et al.) | 2 |
| Alumni mentors as an optional role | Communities of practice; weak ties | 2–3 |
| Course group becomes a read-only archive; members stay in programme and community groups | Weak ties (Rajkumar et al.) | 2 |
| Do not build: public leaderboards, post counts, "seen by" read receipts, forced public posting without a small-group option | Social comparison, shame and face threat | Never |

## WhatsApp wins on habit, but loses on structure, record and privacy

The studies agree on why WhatsApp is popular. Teachers in an early study named **simplicity, no cost, immediacy and "students already use it"** ([Bouhnik & Deshen 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)). Shy students saw other students' questions, peers often answered first, and groups continued as alumni groups. The same teachers listed the costs: **message flooding (worse above 15 students), late-hour messages, pressure to be always available, and exposure to students' personal lives**. In Egypt, a survey of **400 students at Ain Shams, Cairo and Helwan universities** found positive attitudes to WhatsApp for study ([EKB](https://molag.journals.ekb.eg/article_200414_622c8a4f52426546597475ed863394a2.pdf)). In Saudi Arabia, students used it **mostly for announcements and questions, not for learning** ([AWEJ](https://awej.org/students-opinions-on-the-functions-and-usefulness-of-communication-on-whatsapp-in-the-efl-higher-education-context/)). Authors of comparison studies say that students prefer WhatsApp, but that **a threaded forum is still necessary for long discussion** ([UNISA](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/23493?show=full); [SUTD/SAI](https://ftp.saiconference.com/Downloads/Volume10No8/Paper_53-WhatsApp_as_an_Educational_Support_Tool.pdf)). Most of these studies are small and come from one institution. In social terms, WhatsApp is strong at the informal layer: relationships, peer help and the feeling that the cohort is near. It is weak at the structured layer, where the collaboration gains come from. Its harms are mostly harms to boundaries: a fellow on call at all hours, a personal number in a group of strangers, and family and study mixed in one phone.

WhatsApp tools are better than before, but they are still a chat stream. Groups hold **up to 1,024 members** ([GSMArena](https://m.gsmarena.com/whatsapp_unveils_communities_feature_raises_group_limit_to_1024_participants-news-56392.php)). Admins can approve joins ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/03/21/whatsapps-new-feature-gives-admins-an-easier-way-to-control-who-can-join-a-group)). Communities give an announcement group that only admins can post to ([Kanal](https://getkanal.com/blog/community-guide-whatsapp)). The limits for Communities are not confirmed: sources give 2,000 or 5,000 members. A new member sees old messages only if a person chooses to share them, and **the share is limited to 25–100 messages** ([GSMArena](https://www.gsmarena.com/whatsapp_introduces_new_group_message_history_feature-news-71641.php)). A student who joins in week three loses the earlier discussion. The institution never holds the record.

Phone numbers are the largest privacy problem. Today, every member of a WhatsApp group can see every other member's number. WhatsApp announced usernames on 29 June 2026, but **only reservations are open, and the rollout reaches countries "later this year"** ([BleepingComputer](https://bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/whatsapp-rolls-out-usernames-to-help-users-hide-their-phone-number)). No public list says when Egypt gets it. Each user must also turn it on. WhatsApp still needs a phone number to sign up and still collects metadata about who talks to whom ([Smartprix](https://www.smartprix.com/bytes/whatsapp-usernames-are-here-you-can-now-chat-without-sharing-your-phone-number/)).

This matters in Egypt. In 2026 Amnesty documented **dozens of people detained over an online group, among them university students**. Interrogators asked them when and how they joined the group ([Amnesty, July 2026](https://www.amnesty.org/en/latest/news/2026/07/egypt-release-dozens-detained-over-gen-z-online-group-calling-for-political-change/)). That group was on Discord, not WhatsApp. On WhatsApp, the member list and numbers sit on every member's phone. An unlocked, seized phone shows all of them. An in-app feature moves this data to CILAS. CILAS then controls retention, deletion and display names. CILAS also becomes the target for legal requests and has breach duties. Neither option is safe by default, but only the in-app option lets CILAS set the rules.

Egypt's data law now applies. The **Executive Regulations of the PDPL (Law 151/2020) were issued on 1 November 2025**, and the grace period ends around 1 November 2026 or at the end of 2026 ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/are/legal-updates/egypt-s-pdpl-executive-regulations-issued-one-year-compliance-countdown-begins); [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/law_update_articles/from-policy-to-practice-egypt-issues-executive-regulations-of-the-personal-data-protection-law/)). The rules include explicit consent with records, **Arabic privacy notices, defined retention periods, a registered DPO, a 72-hour breach notice, and permits for cross-border transfers**, including cloud hosting. Consent is weak where there is a power imbalance, as between a fellow and a student ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/are/legal-updates/egypt-s-pdpl-executive-regulations-issued-one-year-compliance-countdown-begins)). A course group that CILAS runs on WhatsApp does not remove these duties. It only removes CILAS's control over the data. An in-app feature must be in CILAS's PDPL register, retention policy and privacy notice. The hosting location of the Postgres server also needs a check for transfer rules. This report does not give legal advice; a lawyer must confirm these points.

Facebook Groups is a weaker choice than WhatsApp for course work. It has good admin tools, such as Admin Assist, membership questions and slow mode ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/facebook-adds-a-range-of-new-group-admin-tools-including-an-updated-dashbo/601941)). But students **do not want coursework on their social platform and prefer private tools** for classmates and instructors ([Dennen & Burner 2013](https://spir.aoir.org/ojs/index.php/spir/article/view/8480)). Teachers left Facebook class groups because of friend-request pressure and student exposure ([Bouhnik & Deshen 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)). Meta has also removed group features in the past, such as Rooms, Group Stories and Facebook Campus ([Ubergizmo](https://www.ubergizmo.com/2022/03/facebook-campus-shut-down/)). Facebook still has reach in Egypt, with **51.6M ad reach, but the audience is 60.1% male** ([DataReportal 2026](https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2026-egypt)). For a mixed cohort, that skew is a concern. Arab youth survey data shows **daily Facebook use falling, with WhatsApp first at 82%** ([Arab News](https://www.arabnews.pk/node/2167336)).

## Patterns proven by education platforms give the feature its strength

Education platforms each build around one teaching move. Ed Discussion and Piazza use **questions with endorsed answers, resolved states, and anonymity to peers only**. Staff can always see the author ([Yale Canvas: Ed](https://help.canvas.yale.edu/a/1452322-ed-discussion-creating-and-managing-threads); [Cornell](https://learn.canvas.cornell.edu/comparison-of-discussion-boards-ed-discussion-and-canvas-discussions)). Moodle's Q&A forum makes a student **post before they can see peers' posts**, which reduces anchoring on the first reply ([Swarthmore](https://kb.swarthmore.edu:8443/display/MOODLE/Moodle+Forums)). Canvas "checkpoints" set **one first reply and follow-up replies, each with its own due date** ([PSU](https://oaiplus.pdx.edu/?p=95444)). Google Classroom has **three posting levels per class or per student**, so a fellow can mute one member ([Google](https://support.google.com/edu/classroom/answer/6099424)). Yellowdig "accolades" are badges that **only instructors give**, such as "Insightful" ([UVM](https://www.uvm.edu/it/kb/article/yellowdig/)). Minerva Forum reports **talk time per student** and uses it to balance the next class ([Minerva](https://www.minerva.edu/blog/a-teachers-guide-how-to-use-data-to-measure-student-engagement)). Kialo Edu builds a **tree of pro and con claims under a thesis** ([Blackboard catalog](https://appcatalog.blackboard.com/details/kialo-discussion)). Hypothesis puts discussion **in the margins of a reading** and is open source ([Hypothesis](https://connect.hypothes.is/education/)).

The outcome numbers for these tools are mostly vendor claims. Piazza reports a **median response time of about 25 minutes, with 96% of questions answered** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/?p=478532)). Packback claims nearly 2x more posts per student, but gives no study design ([Packback](https://packback.co/product/student-outcomes)). Packback's own research partner found that **each extra two words added about one point to the AI score** ([Packback/UNT](https://packback.co/pedagogy/unt-research-impact-of-packback-asynchronous-discussion)). Automated quality scores can therefore reward length. CILAS must not use points, leaderboards or AI scores. They are the least proven and do not suit a seminar culture.

## Discourse is the only credible platform, and it still loses on fit

Discourse is the strongest "adopt" option. It is open source, and CILAS can self-host it free of licence fees. Hosted plans are **Free, Pro at $100/month and Business at $500/month, with unlimited members** ([Discourse pricing](https://www.discourse.org/pricing)). An **85% education discount** exists, but it needs proof of a registered degree-granting institution. CILAS is possibly not eligible. The non-profit discount is 50%. DiscourseConnect lets the CILAS app stay the identity provider. The app can push group changes with `add_groups`, `remove_groups` and `sync_sso` when an enrollment changes ([Meta: DiscourseConnect](https://meta.discourse.org/t/setup-discourseconnect-official-single-sign-on-for-discourse-sso/13045)). The protocol is specific to Discourse, so the app needs custom code. It is not clear whether hosted Pro includes it. The only Arabic/RTL source is an old forum thread ([Meta: Arabic](https://meta.discourse.org/t/current-status-of-arabic-text-support/33288)). A hands-on test with Arabic content is necessary before any decision.

The cost of Discourse for CILAS is not the licence. It is a second large system (Ruby, Redis and its own Postgres) for one developer to update and back up. It is also a second design, a second notification system and a second place where members must look. Session threads, endorsed answers in course context, and "promote to article" all need custom glue. A community that moved from Slack to Circle named the same problem: **chat, events and files sat in different tools that members could not find** ([FML Studios](https://fmlstudios.substack.com/p/why-we-migrated-our-community-from)). Maven solved it with a **community built into the course platform** ([Maven help](https://help.maven.com/en/articles/6289166-community-overview)). This is one case study, so it is weak evidence. But it points the same way as the CILAS architecture.

Other options fit less well. Circle and Mighty Networks are for creators who sell memberships. They cost **$79–354+ per month plus transaction fees**, and API and SSO access depend on the plan ([Mighty Networks](https://www.mightynetworks.com/pricing); [Schoolmaker](https://schoolmaker.com/blog/circle-so-pricing)). Zulip is free or cheap for education, but it is a chat tool, and its plans page does not mention RTL ([Zulip plans](https://zulip.com/plans/)). Embedded SDKs are too small when free and too expensive when paid. **Stream Feeds jumps from 5,000 free activities a month to $499/month** ([Stream](https://getstream.io/activity-feeds/pricing/)). **Liveblocks Free has a hard cap of 200 comments** and has no polls, categories or digests ([Liveblocks](https://liveblocks.io/pricing)). Both keep CILAS content outside its Postgres. Education tools such as Ed, Piazza and Perusall connect through LTI, which CILAS does not have. The research found **almost no evidence of Arabic or RTL support** in Ed, Piazza, Perusall, Kialo or Yellowdig. Padlet is the only one with documented Arabic ([Padlet](https://padlet.help/l/en/article/v6iz7bhhl1-untitled-article-klr)). This is a strong reason to build, because CILAS already handles bilingual text and RTL.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## A native build is cheap; adoption and alerts are the real risk

The build is within reach for one developer. The researcher's estimate, which no source confirms, is **3–6 weeks for an MVP** with threads, comments, reactions, polls, fellow moderation and an email digest, and **1–2 weeks more for web push and live updates**. The data model fits Payload: `groups`, `posts`, `comments`, `reactions`, `polls` with options and votes, `reports`, and `subscriptions`. Group membership must come from enrollments and programme memberships through access functions. Do not store a copy of the member list. Polling every 15–30 seconds is enough for discussion that is not chat. Postgres can do search. Its Arabic stemming support is not verified, so use the `simple` config or `pg_trgm` for both languages. Spam risk is low, because only enrolled members can post.

Notifications decide adoption. WhatsApp wins on immediacy, and students do not choose a forum only because it is better for learning. **iOS web push works only for web apps that a user adds to the Home Screen (iOS 16.4 or later)** ([MagicBell](https://www.magicbell.com/blog/pwa-ios-limitations-safari-support-complete-guide)). Many members will not do this step. Email is reliable but slow. The literature gives a practical answer: **a blend, with WhatsApp for quick alerts and the forum for structure and the record** ([Mpungose, via TPACK](https://tpack.pages.wm.edu/archives/129)). One university built a **two-way sync between WhatsApp and the LMS forum**, which helped tutors monitor participation ([IATED 2026](https://library.iated.org/view/JULAEHA2026LEV)). For CILAS, a WhatsApp Channel or a Community announcement group can send short alerts that link back into the app. Channels do not show members' numbers to each other. The alert carries no discussion content. A sync of messages is too much work for one developer and moves data back onto phones.

The fellows are the second risk. The same course design gives **different teaching presence depending on who teaches it** ([IRRODL](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/5456)). If a fellow does not post a weekly prompt and a synthesis, the group will be quiet, whatever the software does. The feature must make the fellow's weekly work fast: a prompt template per session, a reminder before each session, and a one-screen view of who has not posted. The pilot must measure the fellow's time cost.

## Recommendation, phased scope and pilot tests

**Build the feature in the app. Pilot it in two or three courses for one term. Keep a WhatsApp announcement Channel for alerts only.** Do not start the community-wide group until the course groups work. Large open groups suffer most from participation inequality, and the evidence for programme-level belonging is only inference.

| Phase | Scope | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| 0. Preparation (1 week) | Add groups to the PDPL register, privacy notice (Arabic and English) and retention policy. Write a one-page fellow guide: weekly prompt, synthesis, response time. Choose 2–3 pilot courses with willing fellows. Test PWA install and push on Android and iOS. | Lawyer and fellows agree |
| 1. Course groups MVP (3–6 weeks) | One group per course, membership from enrollments. Fellow-opened bilingual introduction thread in week 0. Profiles with optional photo and name form. Small task sub-groups of 3–5 that the fellow makes. Thread per session, made automatically. Post types: prompt (deadline, optional "reply before you see others"), question (endorsed answer, resolved state), discussion, synthesis. Anonymous-to-peers option on questions. Comments, reactions, single-choice polls. Fellow tools: pin, endorse, hide, lock, mute. Report button. In-app notifications plus daily or weekly Resend digest with per-user settings. Quiet hours. Phone numbers never shown. No leaderboards, post counts or read receipts. | Pilot term runs |
| 2. After a successful pilot | Programme and bridge-programme groups (staff moderate, student stewards optional). Fellow participation view (private to the fellow). Fellow badges. Promote thread to article through the review flow. Archive after course end, with a choice of visibility tier; members stay in programme groups. Alumni "how I got through" stories at onboarding. Optional web push for installed PWA users. | Pilot metrics met |
| 3. Later | Community-wide group with alumni. Optional alumni mentor role. Social annotation on readings and articles (evaluate Hypothesis first). | Phase 2 groups active |

The pilot needs clear decision rules. Set them before the term starts, so that the result is honest.

| Decision | Condition |
|---|---|
| Continue to Phase 2 | Most success targets below are met in at least two of the pilot courses. |
| Change and run a second pilot | Students read but do not post, or fellows exceed the time budget. Fix the prompts, notifications or fellow guide first. |
| Stop the build; move to WhatsApp Channel plus a simple forum or Discourse | Fewer than one-third of students open the group each week, and course discussion stays on WhatsApp in all pilot courses. |
| Choose Discourse over the native build | The native MVP is not ready after 8 weeks, or maintenance takes more than about 2 days per month. Run an Arabic/RTL test on Discourse first. |

The targets below are proposals for the pilot. No source sets them. They are calibrated against the 90-9-1 rule, against Piazza response times, and against the design goals of the feature.

| Metric | How to measure | Proposed target |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly readers | Share of enrolled members who open the group or a digest link per week | 70% or more |
| Weekly contributors | Share of enrolled members who post or comment per week | 40% or more (far above 1–25% in open communities) |
| Prompt coverage | Share of sessions with a fellow prompt and a synthesis post | 80% or more |
| Response to questions | Median time to the first reply or fellow endorsement | Under 24 hours |
| Fellow time cost | Self-reported hours per week on the group | 2 hours or less |
| Reach of alerts | Share of members with digest on, or push on an installed PWA | 80% or more |
| Shift from WhatsApp | Fellow and student report of where course questions are asked | Most course questions in the app by week 6 |
| Privacy | Number of phone numbers exposed to peers through course channels | Zero |
| Introductions | Share of members who post in the introduction thread by the end of week 1 | 80% or more |
| Belonging | Short sense-of-belonging survey at week 2 and at course end | Score rises from week 2 to course end; equal to or better than a WhatsApp-only comparison course |
| Social presence | Short social-presence survey at week 2 and at course end | Same rule as belonging |
| Value for learning | End-of-term question on usefulness of the group for the course work | Equal to or better than a WhatsApp-only comparison course |
| Inclusion | Contributor share by gender and by first language (Arabic or English) | No group far below its share of enrollment |
| Knowledge made together | Threads promoted to articles or reused by fellows | At least one per pilot course |

## Conclusion

The question is not "the app or WhatsApp". The research separates two jobs that WhatsApp mixes together. Quick alerts and social glue need reach, and WhatsApp already has it. Discussion that makes knowledge needs design, a record and privacy, and only an owned space gives all three. The social evidence sharpens this split. Belonging and social presence matter, but they pay off for learning only when people meet over shared, structured work. For part-time adults, the Tinto model of social integration is weak, so the social layer must serve the course work, not replace it. The in-app groups can therefore be social on purpose: people visible by name and face, small task groups, and alumni who stay connected. They can also stay free of the boundary costs that WhatsApp brings. CILAS's existing data model makes the second job cheap to build, and its pedagogy gives a reason to build it. A feature that turns a session discussion into a synthesis and then into a published article puts "knowledge made together" into practice. No external platform offers this.

The main uncertainty is human, not technical. The evidence says that fellows, not features, create teaching presence. It also says that people will not leave a habit only because a new tool is better for learning. The pilot must therefore test fellow practice and notification reach as carefully as the code. The pilot must also test the hypothesis that informal chat builds relationships and structured threads build knowledge. No study has yet tested it directly. Several gaps remain open: the real WhatsApp Community limits, the date when usernames reach Egypt, Discourse's current RTL quality, Arabic search in Postgres, and the PDPL position on hosting. Check each one before Phase 2. A bilingual Arabic/English discussion archive would also be unusual in the research literature, so the pilot data can itself become a research asset for CILAS.
