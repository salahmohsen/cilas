# The social dimension of learning: evidence for a CILAS "groups" feature

Scope note: this file does not repeat Community of Inquiry meta-analyses (Martin et al. 2022), Perusall, anonymity, participation grading, participation inequality, Knowledge Forum, or the MDRC learning-communities trial. Those are covered in other notes.

Method note: 15 web searches/fetches. Abstract-level numbers were confirmed in search results. Where I could not see a number in a source during this session, I put it in Gaps. Theory items marked "(foundational; DOI not fetched this session)" are cited from standard bibliographic knowledge. Their claims are standard summaries, not new findings.

## 1. Theory: what do social constructivism, communities of practice, social learning theory, Tinto and SDT predict about peer interaction?

### Takeaway

All five theories predict that peers matter. But they predict _different mechanisms_: (a) talk with a more able other inside the zone of proximal development (Vygotsky); (b) gradual movement from the edge to the centre of a real practice (Lave & Wenger); (c) watching a model who is like you succeed (Bandura); (d) feeling that you are part of the institution (Tinto); (e) the need for relatedness, which feeds motivation (SDT). A groups feature serves (c), (d) and (e) well. It serves (a) and (b) only when real academic work happens inside it and fellows or alumni take part in it. Tinto's social-integration claim has the weakest support for commuter, part-time and adult students, and these are much like the CILAS population.

### Cited Findings

- Vygotsky: learning happens first between people and then inside the learner. The zone of proximal development is the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with guidance from an adult or a "more capable peer". (foundational; DOI not fetched this session) — [Vygotsky 1978, Mind in Society, Harvard UP](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674576292)
- Lave & Wenger: learning is participation in a community of practice. Newcomers start with "legitimate peripheral participation": real but low-risk tasks at the edge. They move toward full participation over time. (foundational; DOI not fetched) — [Lave & Wenger 1991, Situated Learning, CUP](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511815355)
- SDT: autonomy, competence and relatedness are basic needs, and satisfying them supports autonomous motivation. (foundational; DOI not fetched) — [Deci & Ryan 2000, Psychological Inquiry](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327965PLI1104_01)
- SDT online: a 2026 conceptual paper argues that support for autonomy and competence carries over to online settings easily, but "support for relatedness presents unique demands in digital learning environments" — [IATED 2026](https://library.iated.org/view/TADDEO2026REL). A review notes that "varied, research-based relatedness support appropriate for the online learning environment is lacking" — [Heron 2018 via EdITLib](https://mail.editlib.org/p/160227)
- SDT, correlational: two samples of online special-education students (N = 263 and 270). Perceived autonomy, relatedness and competence jointly predicted self-determination. Need satisfaction mediated the link from need support to self-determination — [Chen, UGA dissertation 2009](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/11760/files/chen_kuan-chung_200905_phd.pdf)
- SDT, qualitative: social and context factors online can undermine autonomy, competence and relatedness — [Hartnett 2015, AJET](https://ajet.org.au/index.php/AJET/article/view/1526)
- Tinto: persistence depends on academic integration and social integration into the institution. (foundational; DOI not fetched) — [Tinto 1975, Review of Educational Research](https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543045001089)
- Tests of Tinto: Braxton, Hirschy & McClendon (2004) found empirical support for social integration, but "only modest support" for academic integration on residential campuses. They found "only modest support for either social or academic integration on community college or commuter campuses". Academic integration had strong _indirect_ effects at commuter institutions — [summary of Braxton et al. 2004/2014, MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/553666/pdf)
- Braxton & Lee (2005), seen only through a secondary citation: none of 13 testable propositions held for commuter institutions. Those students "have less developed social communities" and have family and work obligations. Braxton et al. (2014) built a separate commuter model. It starts from entry traits (motivation, self-efficacy, "anticipatory socialization"), not from integration — [Braxton et al. 2014 via WIT repository](https://repository.wit.ie/7812/1/TD_IETI.pdf)

### Inferences

- For part-time adult learners, the classic "social integration → persistence" path is weak. The better lever is probably _academic_ social contact: peers who discuss the readings and the work. Social contact for its own sake does less. So a groups feature should put course work at the centre, not only general community chat.
- Legitimate peripheral participation suggests that new members need low-risk entry tasks, for example reacting, answering a poll, or posting a short introduction, before they write substantive posts. It also suggests a path toward the centre: alumni → mentors → fellows.
- Bandura's modelling (Social Learning Theory, 1977; I fetched no source this session) predicts that seeing peers _like oneself_ work through difficulty helps self-efficacy. This supports showing alumni stories and showing peers' work in progress.

### Gaps

- I did not fetch a primary source for Bandura's social learning theory or for its effect sizes in online peer settings.
- I found no meta-analysis of SDT relatedness specifically in online higher education.

## 2. Sense of belonging: links to motivation, persistence and achievement, with subgroup effects

### Takeaway

Belonging correlates modestly with academic outcomes (pooled r ≈ 0.20) and more strongly with well-being (r ≈ 0.40). The studies vary widely and many have a high risk of bias. The best causal evidence comes from brief belonging interventions. They produce small but real gains in persistence (about 2 percentage points), only for groups that historically persist at lower rates, and only where the setting actually offers chances to belong. Belonging is a property of the setting as well as of the person.

### Cited Findings

- 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis: university sense of belonging correlates with academic outcomes at r = 0.20 (small to medium) and with well-being at r = 0.40. Heterogeneity was high and most studies had a high risk of bias. Correlational. — [Springer 2025, Australian Educational Researcher](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13384-025-00822-8)
- Scoping review (Fong et al.): 69 reports, 78 samples, 2003–2023. Associations of belonging with GPA, persistence and intent to persist were mostly positive but small, and some were small and negative — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/a-scoping-review-of-the-associations-between-sense-of-belonging-a/)
- Walton & Cohen 2011 (Science), RCT: 92 first-year students (49 Black, 43 white). A ~1-hour belonging exercise reframed adversity as common and temporary. It raised Black students' GPA over 3 years and halved the minority achievement gap. Self-reported health also improved and doctor visits fell. It had no effect for white students. The sample was small. — [Walton & Cohen 2011, Science (Ovid)](https://www.ovid.com/journals/scie/pdf/00007529-201103180-00038~a-brief-social-belonging-intervention-improves-academic-and); [WWC review](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/Study/81609)
- Walton et al. 2023 (Science 380:499–505), RCT: 26,911 students at 22 institutions. A pre-college online exercise of under 30 minutes raised full-time first-year completion, mainly for groups that historically persisted at lower rates. The effect appeared "only when students' groups were afforded opportunities to belong". In supportive settings, completion for those groups rose by about 2 percentage points — [PSU Pure abstract](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/where-and-with-whom-does-a-brief-social-belonging-intervention-pr/); [Stanford News](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2023/05/improving-student-success-social-belonging)
- Commentary on the 2023 paper also asks whether these interventions work at scale — [Psychology Today, 2023](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202305/do-belonging-interventions-for-college-students-work/)
- Single study (n = 578): students who often thought about dropping out reported lower belonging. Correlational. — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/a-sense-of-belonging-at-university-student-retention-motivation-a/)
- Belonging and socio-economic status in higher education: a quantitative study — [Goldsmiths](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/36489)

### Inferences

- The "affordances" finding from 2023 matters for design. A belonging message helps only if the environment then delivers real chances to connect. A groups feature can be that delivery mechanism: per-course introductions, small working groups, and fellow presence. But a groups feature with no activity may signal non-belonging.
- Effects concentrate in students who doubt that they belong (first-generation, minority, international and non-traditional students). CILAS students from several countries, older adults and non-degree students may be in this group, so CILAS could use a "stories from earlier cohorts" onboarding item (alumni say "I also felt out of place at first").

### Gaps

- I found no meta-analytic effect size specifically for adult, part-time or online learners' belonging.
- I did not confirm the full-sample effect in Walton et al. 2023.
- I did not access Strayhorn's work (College Students' Sense of Belonging) in this session.

## 3. Social presence: definitions and effect sizes

### Takeaway

Social presence (the ability to project yourself as a "real person" in a mediated setting) correlates strongly with satisfaction and perceived learning in fully online courses. The evidence is correlational and uses self-report outcomes. Course length, discipline and audience moderate the relationship. It is not evidence that social presence raises _measured_ achievement.

### Cited Findings

- Richardson, Maeda, Lv & Caskurlu (2017), Computers in Human Behavior 71:402–417. Meta-analysis of 25 studies (1992–2015), fully online higher education. It reports a strong positive relationship of social presence with satisfaction and with perceived learning. For satisfaction, course length, discipline and the social presence scale were significant moderators. For perceived learning, course length, discipline and audience were moderators — [ETHE table summarising it](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-022-00355-5/tables/1); [Caskurlu dissertation, Purdue](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI30502685)
- A 2025 meta-analysis (studies from 1995–2022) in the International Journal of Distance Education reports a _moderate_ effect of social presence on both satisfaction and learning outcomes. This is a smaller estimate than the 2017 "strong" wording — [Érudit / IJEDE 2025](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/ijede/2025-v40-n1-ijede010122/1118768ar)
- Instructor presence in Arab online discussion: a study compared instructor-posting and peer-only forums with 52 master's students at Albaha University, Saudi Arabia, and measured participation, social presence and writing quality. I did not see its results — [Shamaa record](https://search.shamaa.org/FullRecord?ID=217946)

### Inferences

- Satisfaction and perceived learning predict whether people stay and come back, which is relevant for non-degree adult learners. But do not claim that social features raise grades on the basis of social-presence evidence alone.
- The moderator "course length" suggests that social presence matters more in longer courses. Short CILAS courses may need faster, scripted "get to know each other" moves.

### Gaps

- I could not access the exact pooled correlations for Richardson et al. 2017. My recollection is r ≈ 0.5, but I did not verify it, so it should not be cited.
- I did not fetch primary sources for Short, Williams & Christie (1976) or Gunawardena (1995/1997).

## 4. Peer and collaborative learning meta-analyses

### Takeaway

Structured peer and collaborative learning has robust, moderate positive effects: d ≈ 0.4–0.6 on achievement, and in SMET small-group learning, ≈ 0.46 on persistence. In CSCL, collaboration produces knowledge gains of ≈ 0.42 and skill gains of ≈ 0.64, and scaffolds and supporting strategies add more. The key word is _structured_. The effects come from designed tasks, not from the presence of a chat channel.

### Cited Findings

- Chen, Wang, Kirschner & Tsai (2018), Review of Educational Research 88(6):799–843: 425 empirical studies, 2000–2016. Collaboration in computer settings: knowledge gain g = 0.42, skill acquisition 0.64, student perceptions 0.38. Computer use within collaboration: knowledge 0.45, skills 0.53, perceptions 0.51, group task performance 0.89, social interaction 0.57. Extra tools or environments: knowledge 0.55. Supporting strategies (scripts, scaffolds): knowledge 0.38 — [OU NL record](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/the-role-of-collaboration-computer-use-learning-environments-and-/); [EdWeek summary](https://edweek.org/technology/computers-collaboration-student-learning-according-to-new-meta-analysis/2018/11)
- Jeong et al. (2019), a STEM CSCL meta-analysis: overall effect 0.51 across 143 studies, 2005–2014 — [IU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/dspace/items/29b10300-a2be-40ab-9ff5-c6585efe9020)
- Springer, Stanne & Donovan (1999), RER 69(1):21–51, undergraduate SMET since 1980: small-group learning gives greater achievement, more favourable attitudes and greater persistence. Effect sizes as reported in a secondary source: achievement 0.51, persistence 0.46, attitudes 0.55. The persistence effect would reduce attrition by about 22%. A physics-education paper cites an N-weighted d = 0.57 for achievement (31 studies, 2,559 students), so the figures differ by weighting — [Springer et al. PDF](https://www2.math.upenn.edu/%7Epemantle/active-papers/springer-stanne-donovan.pdf); [ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/cooperative-learning); [Hake, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0106087)
- Hattie (Visible Learning, 2017 list): cooperative vs individualistic learning d = 0.55; peer tutoring 0.53; cooperative vs competitive 0.53. 2009 appendix: 0.59, 0.55, 0.54; small-group learning 0.49. Hattie's hinge point is about 0.40. Note: Hattie's method (averaging meta-analyses) has known critiques — [visible-learning.org](https://visible-learning.org/hattie-ranking-influences-effect-sizes-learning-achievement/); [Corwin appendix](https://resources.corwin.com/sites/default/files/appendix_4.pdf)

### Inferences

- The gains need a task with a goal, interdependence and individual accountability (the Johnson & Johnson conditions; I fetched no source this session). A free-form group chat does not meet these conditions. So the groups feature should support _task-shaped_ threads: fellow-posted prompts, small-group assignments, polls tied to a reading, and summaries.
- The CSCL "supporting strategies" effect (0.38 _on top of_ collaboration) argues for built-in scaffolds such as structured post types (question / claim / evidence / build-on).

### Gaps

- I did not retrieve a Hattie entry for "classroom discussion" (often cited as ~0.82) from a source this session, so I do not cite it.
- I did not fetch Johnson & Johnson meta-analysis numbers directly.

## 5. Loneliness, isolation, dropout, and social ties in MOOCs

### Takeaway

Isolation is widely _named_ as a driver of online dropout. The strongest synthesis (296 studies) says belonging, self-efficacy and regular interaction with instructors and peers reduce isolation. But the causal evidence is thin. In MOOCs, socially active learners complete more often (selection is likely). An experiment that put learners into groups and introduced them did _not_ raise completion. Assigning a group is not enough; groups need structure and a reason to exist.

### Cited Findings

- Isolation and disconnectedness are often named as the two main factors in online dropout, and attrition is often attributed to a lack of interaction. These claims are often asserted rather than tested — [IRRODL 2012](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/download/1171/2206?inline=1)
- Canadian systematic review (SSHRC knowledge synthesis), 296 studies, 2012–2022: belonging to a community, self-efficacy, and regular social interaction with instructors and peers affect a distance learner's sense of isolation. It recommends instructor training and teaching online interaction skills early — [SSHRC evidence brief](https://www.sshrc-crsh.gc.ca/society-societe/community-communite/ifca-iac/evidence_briefs-donnees_probantes/ksg_evidence_briefs/papi-eng.aspx)
- Counter-signal: a 2020 study found that students did not feel predominantly isolated in distance learning — [European Proceedings 2020](https://europeanproceedings.com/pdf/article/10.15405/epsbs.2020.03.03.64)
- Older work: attrition among distance learners was historically higher than among on-campus learners, and communication tools can address loneliness — [IRRODL 2003](https://erudit.org/en/journals/irrodl/2003-v4-n1-irrodl05609/1072834ar.pdf)
- MOOCs (French project-management MOOC, Cisel 2018): learners with high social interaction succeed more. Interactions _outside_ the forums (friends and family taking the MOOC together) are under-studied but may matter for retention. Many learners take MOOCs as an activity with people they already know — [IRRODL 2018, "Interactions in MOOCs: the hidden part of the iceberg"](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/3459)
- Experimental counter-evidence (ETR&D): placing MOOC learners in groups based on their preferences and introducing them to each other did not improve performance or completion. The authors suggest teaching people how to work as group members — reported in [Mercy GER review](https://ger.mercy.edu/index.php/ger/article/view/124/138)
- Systematic review 2024: social interactions in MOOCs fall into three types: discussion forums, learning groups, and one-to-one peer interaction — [Frontiers in Education 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1345205/pdf)

### Inferences

- CILAS small cohorts are not MOOCs. Small size and a named fellow make real ties more likely. The MOOC null result warns against expecting "join group" to work by itself.
- The Cisel finding (people study with people they already know) suggests a feature to _bring a friend_, or to let existing pairs stay together across courses.

### Gaps

- I found no causal study showing that peer ties raise MOOC completion. The positive links are correlational.
- I found no quantitative study on loneliness among adult part-time learners in MENA.

## 6. Weak ties, social capital and alumni networks

### Takeaway

The strongest causal evidence on networks (LinkedIn, 20M+ users, randomised) shows that _moderately weak_ ties produce the most job mobility, in an inverted-U pattern. In less digital industries, strong ties mattered more. A cohort and alumni network can create this kind of capital, but only if it stays connected after the course ends and spans cohorts.

### Cited Findings

- Granovetter: weak ties carry new information between dense clusters. (foundational; DOI not fetched) — [Granovetter 1973, AJS](https://doi.org/10.1086/225469)
- Rajkumar, Saint-Jacques, Bojinov, Brynjolfsson & Aral (2022), Science 377:1304–1310. Randomised variation in the "People You May Know" recommender for over 20 million users over 5 years. Tie strength and job transmission follow an inverted U. Moderately weak ties (by mutual connections) and the weakest ties (by interaction intensity) produced the most job mobility. Weak ties helped more in digital industries; strong ties helped more in less digital industries — [Science PDF via gwern](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/2022-rajkumar.pdf); [MIT IDE](https://ide.mit.edu/insights/new-publication-mit-harvard-and-stanford-scientists-show-weaker-ties-are-more-beneficial-for-job-seekers-on-linkedin/)
- Ethical caveat: the experiment ran without explicit user consent and drew criticism — [ACS Information Age](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2022/secret-study-conducted-on-20-million-linkedin-users.html)

### Inferences

- A community-wide group (across courses, cohorts and alumni) is the layer that creates bridging weak ties. Per-course groups create bonding strong ties. CILAS needs both. A design that archives a course group at course end and keeps people in programme and community groups preserves the weak ties.
- In Egypt, many cultural and NGO jobs move through personal referral. This may fit the "strong ties in less digital industries" finding, but that is speculation.

### Gaps

- I found no study of alumni networks of non-degree liberal-arts programmes, and none of MENA alumni networks.

## 7. Downsides of the social layer, and cultural factors in Arab and MENA classrooms

### Takeaway

Social layers can bring overload, an "always on" teacher, resistance to teacher demands in informal channels, shame and confrontation avoidance (reported by Gulf students online), and gender-norm limits on mixed discussion. Some Arab-context evidence suggests that online text spaces can _help_ women and shy students take part. So the design choice is about norms, visibility and boundaries, not only about "social or not".

### Cited Findings

- Al-Harthi (2005), phenomenological study, n = 6 Gulf students in US distance programmes: themes were less participation, persistent feelings of social shame online, language difficulty, and avoidance of confrontation. Framed with Hofstede and Hall's high-context culture — [IRRODL 2005](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/263)
- Tlili et al. (2021), 116 Tunisian students, 6-week online course, lag-sequential analysis: culture affected how students engaged in online discussions. The authors suggest online learning may benefit female students who face social pressure in Arab settings — [Sustainability 2021 via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jsusta/v13y2021i22p12426-d676202.html)
- Saudi context: gender segregation extends to universities. Anonymous online forums allow cross-gender talk that need not break norms, but online conventions remain a "gray area" — [SAGE, Convergence 2014, DOI 10.1177/1354856514534574](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1354856514534574) (this source was surfaced via a proxy link; check its provenance)
- Bouhnik & Deshen (2014), 12 teacher interviews, Israel: WhatsApp class groups are used to communicate, build social atmosphere, encourage sharing, and as a learning platform. Benefits named: immediacy, low cost, a pleasant social atmosphere, and learning beyond class hours. Drawbacks: a high volume of irrelevant messages, and an expectation that teachers are available 24 hours a day — [JITE 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)
- Israeli high-school studies: students value WhatsApp's easy access, communal nature and privacy boundaries, but report communication overload. Students resist teachers' demands on WhatsApp more than demands made in class — [Asterhan et al., HUJI](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/whatsapp-teacher-student-perspectives-on-teacher-student-whatsapp-2/); [Chais 2019](https://www.openu.ac.il/innovation/chais2019/f/c1_11.pdf)

### Inferences

- Design responses for MENA norms: profiles that let users control photo and name display; fellow-moderated spaces; norms that model disagreement politely (to lower face threat); polls and reactions as low-risk entry points; private small groups; no forced mixed-gender video. Text-first spaces may widen participation for women and shy students.
- WhatsApp's main harm is to _boundaries_ (24/7 teacher, personal number exposed, mixing family and study). A CILAS-owned space can set quiet hours, keep phone numbers hidden, and keep fellows off call.

### Gaps

- I found no robust quantitative study of face-saving or teacher authority in online discussion in Egyptian higher education.
- The search on social comparison, fear of judgement and harassment in course groups (beyond the WhatsApp overload findings) was not done within the search budget.

## 8. Informal vs formal social spaces (WhatsApp/Facebook vs structured forums)

### Takeaway

Evidence from Arab universities shows that WhatsApp is heavily used and preferred for study support, coordination and peer help, and an instructor-run WhatsApp group can improve outcomes (vocabulary). Studies of instructor-led groups dominate this evidence. I found no head-to-head study showing that informal chat builds relationships while structured forums build knowledge. That claim stays a plausible hypothesis drawn from theory and from the CSCL evidence on structure.

### Cited Findings

- Saudi survey (~200 students and staff): WhatsApp is heavily used for educational support and "greatly preferred" over other platforms — [SAI conference paper](https://ftp.saiconference.com/Downloads/Volume10No8/Paper_53-WhatsApp_as_an_Educational_Support_Tool.pdf)
- Saudi EFL (qualitative): WhatsApp was used for information exchange, language support and practice through teacher-directed, peer and autonomous learning. Views were mostly positive, but some faculty had reservations — [UNE repository](https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/22769)
- Jordan, quasi-experiment (Al-Balqa Applied University): a researcher-run WhatsApp vocabulary group beat a conventional-teaching control on the post-test — [TPLS](https://tpls.academypublication.com/index.php/tpls/article/view/8889)
- Bouhnik & Deshen: teachers use WhatsApp groups both to "nurture the social atmosphere" and as a learning platform. The same channel mixes both jobs, which drives the noise problem — [JITE 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)

### Inferences

- A sound hybrid: one channel for low-stakes social chat (reactions, introductions, logistics) and separate threaded, task-shaped discussion for academic work. These can be two spaces in the same app rather than WhatsApp plus an LMS. That keeps the relationship benefit and avoids burying course content.
- The in-app product competes with a default (WhatsApp) that students already prefer. It must give clear value that WhatsApp lacks: threads, searchable archive, attached readings, fellow presence, privacy of numbers, persistence after the course, and links across cohorts.

### Gaps

- I found no controlled comparison of Facebook or WhatsApp groups against LMS forums on learning outcomes.

## 9. Design implications for a CILAS groups feature

### Takeaway

Build for _structured academic interaction plus visible people_. Do not build for generic chat. The highest-yield features, by the evidence above: introductions and profiles (social presence), fellow presence (instructor social presence and modelling), small task groups with scaffolds (CSCL g ≈ 0.4–0.6), alumni stories at onboarding (belonging intervention logic), and persistent programme and community groups (weak ties). Main risks: empty groups, overload, boundary collapse, and culturally costly public exposure.

### Cited Findings (evidence that supports each implication)

- Structure beats presence: collaboration effects come with designed tasks; supporting strategies add g = 0.38 — [Chen et al. 2018](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/the-role-of-collaboration-computer-use-learning-environments-and-/)
- Groups alone do not raise MOOC completion — [Mercy GER review](https://ger.mercy.edu/index.php/ger/article/view/124/138)
- Belonging messages work only where real chances to belong exist — [Walton et al. 2023](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/where-and-with-whom-does-a-brief-social-belonging-intervention-pr/)
- Social presence links to satisfaction and perceived learning — [Richardson et al. 2017 via ETHE table](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-022-00355-5/tables/1)
- Overload and the 24/7 teacher expectation in WhatsApp groups — [Bouhnik & Deshen 2014](https://www.jite.org/documents/Vol13/JITEv13ResearchP217-231Bouhnik0601.pdf)
- Shame and confrontation avoidance among Gulf learners — [Al-Harthi 2005](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/263)
- Moderately weak ties produce the most opportunity — [Rajkumar et al. 2022](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/sociology/technology/2022-rajkumar.pdf)

### Inferences (concrete feature list)

- **Build:** a bilingual intro thread opened by the fellow in week 0, with a prompt such as "what question brings you here"; profiles with an optional photo and a pronoun or Arabic name form; fellow posts that model tentative thinking; small sub-groups of 3–5 for tasks; structured post types; polls tied to readings (low-risk entry); reactions (peripheral participation); end-of-week digest instead of push spam; quiet hours; hidden phone numbers; alumni "how I got through" stories at onboarding; alumni mentors as an optional role; course groups that become read-only archives and roll members into programme and community groups.
- **Avoid or limit:** public leaderboards or post counts (social comparison); "seen by" read receipts; mandatory public posting in mixed groups with no private or small-group alternative; unbounded community-wide feeds that bury course work; any expectation that fellows answer at all hours.
- **Measure:** a short belonging and social-presence pulse at week 2 and at the end, plus completion. That lets CILAS test whether the feature works for its own population.

### Gaps

- No study tests these specific feature combinations in MENA adult liberal-arts settings, so CILAS should pilot and measure.
- Evidence on reactions, likes and recognition badges as builders of belonging in course communities was not retrieved within the search budget.
