# Learning-science evidence on online course discussion, peer learning communities, and why forums succeed or fail

Scope note: research done on 2026-10-20 with about 15 search/fetch calls. Many primary papers were reached only through abstracts or secondary summaries; this is marked where it applies. Figures marked "secondary" need a check against the original paper before publication.

## 1. Community of Inquiry (CoI): teaching presence, structured prompts and discussion quality

### Takeaway

Across meta-analyses, teaching presence (design, facilitation, direct instruction) correlates moderately with actual learning (r ≈ .35) and strongly with satisfaction (r ≈ .51). Cognitive presence correlates most with perceived learning. Quasi-experimental work shows that structure (role assignment, regulation scaffolds) raises the level of cognitive presence in asynchronous discussion. Most of this evidence is correlational, so the case is "facilitated, structured discussion is associated with better outcomes", not proof of cause.

### Cited Findings

- Martin, Wu, Wan & Xie (2022), _Online Learning_: meta-analysis of 19 empirical studies on the CoI presences in online and blended settings. Teaching presence correlated with actual learning r = .353, with perceived learning r = .392, and with satisfaction r = .510. Cognitive presence correlated with perceived learning r = .663 (the strongest link reported). These are pooled correlations, not causal effects. — [Martin et al. 2022, OLJ](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/2604); [PDF](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/download/2604/1161)
- Caskurlu, Maeda, Richardson & Lv (2020), _Computers & Education_ 157:103966: meta-analysis of 30 studies (1997–2018) of fully online courses. A secondary source reports a "moderately strong positive relationship" between teaching presence and satisfaction, and says each of the three teaching-presence dimensions (design and organisation, facilitation, direct instruction) predicted outcomes. Exact coefficients not retrieved (secondary). — [Review table citing Caskurlu et al. 2020](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-022-00355-5/tables/1)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of social presence in higher-education online settings exists (_Journal of Distance Education / IJEDE_ vol. 40). Effect sizes not retrieved. — [IJEDE 2025](https://ijede.ca/index.php/jde/article/view/1351)
- A study in IRRODL found that the individual instructor accounts for differences in teaching-presence scores across online course sections (that is, the same course design gives different presence depending on who teaches it). Details not retrieved. — [IRRODL](https://www.irrodl.org/index.php/irrodl/article/view/5456)
- Gašević, Adesope, Joksimović & Kovanović (2015), _Internet and Higher Education_ 24:53–65: quasi-experiment, 82 students in a fully online course. Both externally facilitated regulation scaffolding and role assignment had significant positive effects on the level of cognitive presence; role assignment alone was effective; the two strategies interacted significantly. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Externally-facilitated-regulation-scaffolding-and-role-assignment/99900601054601842)
- Systematic review of strategies to foster cognitive presence (2008–2020, _Online Learning_ 2022): the most used strategies are instructor facilitation/scaffolding, followed by structured, problem-based and role-based designs. — [OLJ systematic review](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/3071)
- Counter-evidence: a study of online case-based discussion found that assigning roles alone did not improve knowledge construction, engagement or interaction. — [GMU ITLCP](https://journals.gmu.edu/ITLCP/article/view/640)
- A systematic review of asynchronous discussion found most student posts sit in the "exploration" and "integration" phases of cognitive presence, with few reaching "resolution"; most reviewed studies were US-based. (secondary, via search summary) — [Regis/IGI chapter on cognitive presence strategies](https://epublications.regis.edu/facultypubs/745)

### Inferences

- An in-app group with a fellow as moderator gives the institution a place to exercise all three teaching-presence components (design of prompts, facilitation, direct instruction). A WhatsApp group mainly supports facilitation and offers little support for design (prompts, roles, threads).
- Discussion features that support structure (pinned prompt per week, roles such as "summariser" or "devil's advocate", a "wrap-up" post) map to the interventions with the best evidence. Polls and reactions are not directly supported by this literature.
- Because discussions tend to stall before "resolution", a feature that asks the fellow or a student to write a synthesis (resolution) post is a design response to a known weakness.

### Gaps

- Exact Caskurlu et al. (2020) coefficients were not retrieved.
- No CoI meta-analysis isolates the effect of the platform (LMS forum vs. chat app).

## 2. Meta-analyses and major studies: asynchronous discussion and learning, critical thinking, belonging, retention

### Takeaway

No retrieved quantitative meta-analysis gives a pooled effect size for "asynchronous discussion → critical thinking". The best syntheses are qualitative and say that critical thinking in discussion needs a multi-step, deliberately facilitated design. Evidence on belonging and retention comes mostly from the learning-communities literature (Section 8), where effects are real but modest.

### Cited Findings

- DiPasquale & Hunter (2018), _Canadian Journal of Learning and Technology_: qualitative systematic review of strategies for critical thinking and reflection in fully online higher education. No pooled effect sizes. Main conclusion: practitioners should use a multi-step approach to facilitate critical thinking and reflection in asynchronous discussion. — [CJLT](https://journals.library.ualberta.ca/cjlt/index.php/cjlt/article/view/27782)
- A study of 103 university students examined how group size in asynchronous discussion affects interaction, participation and critical thinking (effect sizes not in the retrieved text). — [iJET](https://online-journals.org/index.php/i-jet/article/download/9351/5522/32212)
- Rovai (2003), _Journal of Computing in Higher Education_: 18 graduate courses, N = 262, Blackboard. Grading strategies influenced online discussions, and discussion was related to students' sense of community. — [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FBF02940854)
- In MOOCs, interaction with instructors emerged as an important factor for more assignment completions, in addition to forum posting (PACIS 2016 / J. Global Information Management 2018; secondary). — [Nottingham Ningbo record](https://research.nottingham.edu.cn/en/publications/role-of-instructors-forum-interactions-with-students-in-promoting/)

### Inferences

- The defensible claim for CILAS is: discussion supports learning when it is designed and facilitated, not merely when it exists. A groups feature must be paired with fellow practice (prompts, synthesis), or it will not produce the gains.

### Gaps

- No quantitative meta-analysis of asynchronous discussion and critical thinking was found in this search. A follow-up search on "meta-analysis computer-supported collaborative learning" (e.g. Chen, Wang, Kirschner & Tsai 2018, _Review of Educational Research_) would probably give pooled CSCL effect sizes; not verified here.
- No retrieved study links forum participation directly to retention in small-cohort settings.

## 3. Social annotation (Perusall, Hypothesis)

### Takeaway

The headline Perusall study shows high reading completion (about 90–95% of students completing nearly all assignments) and exam scores 4–5 percentage points higher (effect size ≈ 0.31–0.34) than a cohort using a simpler annotation tool. But the design is a non-randomised cohort comparison and the authors are Perusall's founders and an employee. Independent reviews describe social annotation as promising, with mostly descriptive evidence.

### Cited Findings

- Miller, Lukoff, King & Mazur (2018), _Frontiers in Education_, Harvard Applied Physics 50 (flipped calculus-based physics). Perusall cohorts: 74 (spring 2015) and 79 (fall 2016); comparison cohorts with an earlier, non-social annotation tool: 72 and 75. — [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2018.00008/full)
  - Reading completion: about 60% completed all 17 assignments; about 90% (spring 2015) and 95% (fall 2016) completed all but a couple; about 80% read at least 95% of pages before class. Students averaged 13.3 annotations per assignment, about twice the requirement, and about 3 h 20 min of reading per week.
  - Exams: Perusall students scored 5–10% better on most exams. Fall: 43% vs 38% (effect size 0.34, p < .05). Spring: 45% vs 41% (effect size 0.31, p < .05). Baseline concept-inventory scores did not differ (p = .32, .36).
  - Regression (N = 79): prior physics knowledge was the strongest predictor (β ≈ .65–.67); time per chapter was not significant; number of reading sessions was (β = .41, p < .01; R² from .43 to .51).
  - Caveats: the authors say the cohort difference does not establish cause. Conflict of interest: the authors are co-founders of Perusall LLC and Lukoff is a Perusall employee. Treat as vendor-affiliated.
- Introductory programming study (arXiv 2024): of 62 students who completed fewer than two reading assignments, 64.5% (40) failed the exam, about three times the failure rate (22%) of students who completed at least two. Many students passed without much annotation, so completion alone does not explain outcomes. Correlational. — [arXiv 2407.10322](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.10322)
- Zhu, Chen, Avadhanam, Shui & Zhang (2020), _Information and Learning Sciences_ 121(5/6): review of 39 articles on web annotation in formal education. Five activity designs: domain knowledge processing, argumentation and inquiry, literacy skills, instructor and peer assessment, and connecting online learning spaces. Recommends clear pedagogical goals and instructor involvement. Note: rapid-response special issue, not double-blind peer reviewed. — [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/ILS-04-2020-0117/full/html); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/reading-and-connecting-using-social-annotation-in-online-classes/)
- Ghadirian, Salehi & Ayub (2018), _International Journal of Learning Technology_: preliminary systematic review of 71 studies on social annotation tools in higher education. — [Inderscience](https://www.inderscience.com/offers.php?id=92096)
- _Research in Learning Technology_ (ALT) study on students' perceptions of social annotation and its relation to grades. — [RLT](https://journal.alt.ac.uk/index.php/rlt/article/view/3050)
- A Monash University online postgraduate study reports that pre-class social annotations had a positive effect on post-class assessment performance (secondary summary). — [UFL instructor guide summarising studies](https://ufl.pb.unizin.org/instructorguide/chapter/annotated-reading-perusall/)

### Inferences

- For a reading-heavy liberal-arts curriculum, discussion anchored to the text (comments on a passage) has better evidence for reading completion than open-ended threads. A group feature that can link a post to a reading or a passage keeps discussion close to course work.
- The "number of sessions" finding suggests that frequent, short returns to the material matter. Light, timely notifications that pull students back may help, but this is an inference.

### Gaps

- No independent randomised trial of Perusall or Hypothesis was found. Hypothesis's own research pages were not reviewed in this pass.

## 4. Anonymous vs identified posting

### Takeaway

Evidence is thin and mostly from computing courses. When anonymity is available, women use it more, which may help them post but can also hide their presence from peers and so weaken their sense of belonging. Anonymity raises willingness to post on sensitive topics.

### Cited Findings

- "Anonymity: A Double-Edged Sword for Gender Equity in a CS1 Forum?": in a CS1 course with an even gender split, women posted anonymously more often than men. The authors argue this disguised women's presence, so students misperceived the gender distribution of the class and women lost chances to see that they belong. — [OLC conference abstract](https://conference.onlinelearningconsortium.org/node/12342)
- ICER 2023, "Engagement and Anonymity in Online Computer Science Course Forums": examines anonymity and engagement; also reports that different ways of collecting gender data change how over 20% of students are classified. — [ICER 2023](https://icer2023.acm.org/details/icer-2023-papers/4/Engagement-and-Anonymity-in-Online-Computer-Science-Course-Forums)
- Online experiment, 466 undergraduate women: an anonymous forum increased intention to post sensitive (sex-related) content and perceived appropriateness of such posts. — [St Thomas research record](https://researchonline.stthomas.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Young-Womens-Sex-Talk-Online-Roles/991015131891803691)
- An engineering forum study found female students significantly more likely to take part in online discussion than male students (identified posting). — [Purdue engineering forum study](https://www.purdue.edu/freeform/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/The-Relationship-Between-Demographic-Characteristics-and-Engagement-in-an-Undergraduate-Engineering-Online-Forum.pdf)
- Joyner and colleagues (Georgia Tech OMSCS) studied forum participation by demographic group. — [Joyner paper PDF](https://www.davidjoyner.net/home/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/3478431.3499289.pdf)

### Inferences

- A good compromise for CILAS: identified posting by default (builds cohort identity), with an "anonymous to classmates, visible to the fellow" option for questions. This is how Piazza-style course forums work; the evidence above supports both the benefit and the cost.

### Gaps

- No found study on anonymity effects for Arabic-speaking or MENA students, or on civility outcomes of anonymity in course forums.

## 5. Participation grading and incentives

### Takeaway

Grading with explicit quality criteria increases both the amount and the depth of peer interaction. Grading on post counts alone tends to raise quantity. Reviews call grading and required post counts "dilemmas" with mixed results.

### Cited Findings

- Swan, Shen & Hiltz (2006/2007), reported in _e-mentor_: across 8 sections of an undergraduate course, the group graded on explicit quality criteria responded significantly more often and at greater length to classmates and read significantly more classmates' posts. — [e-mentor](https://www.e-mentor.edu.pl/artykul/index/numer/18/id/390)
- Rovai (2003): N = 262 in 18 graduate courses; grading strategy influenced discussion activity, and discussion related to sense of community. — [Unpaywall record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FBF02940854)
- Hew, Cheung & Ng (2010), _Instructional Science_ 38(6):571–606: review of 50 empirical studies on limited student contribution. Treats grading, required post counts and instructor facilitation as "dilemmas" with mixed empirical results. Citing work reports that posting deadlines increased contributions. — [HKU repository](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/194283)
- Solan & Linardopoulos (2011), _MERLOT JOLT_: proposes a rubric (quantity, quality, timeliness, writing) and notes the lack of research on grading online discussion. — [JOLT](https://jolt.merlot.org/vol7no4/linardopoulos_1211.pdf)

### Inferences

- For course groups, the feature should let fellows set a weekly prompt with a deadline and see each student's contributions in one place (for quality-based feedback), not only counts. Reactions/"likes" are not a substitute for quality criteria.

### Gaps

- No retrieved study directly compares graded vs ungraded discussion on learning outcomes (as opposed to activity).

## 6. Why course forums die or stay quiet, and what revives them

### Takeaway

Participation is very unequal by default (about 1% of members create most content). Instructor behaviour matters, but more instructor posting is not always better: frequent instructor replies can shorten student threads, while instructor-initiated posts help more, especially in larger classes. Students prefer chat apps for immediacy, but LMS forums keep value for longer discussion, records and oversight.

### Cited Findings

- Nielsen (2006), "Participation Inequality": in most online communities 90% lurk, 9% contribute a little, 1% create almost all activity; more skewed for blogs (95-5-0). Advice: make contribution easy, reward participation without over-rewarding. Original NN/g page not fetched; seen via reproductions. — [Reproduction of Nielsen's Alertbox](https://churbuck.com/2006/10/31/participation-inequality-lurkers-vs-contributors-in-internet-communities-jakob-nielsens-alertbox/); [P2P Foundation summary](https://wiki.p2pfoundation.net/Participation_Inequality)
- van Mierlo (2014), _JMIR_ 16(2):e33: four moderated digital-health networks, 63,990 actors, 578,349 posts. Fewer than 25% of actors ever posted. Lurkers, contributors and superusers produced 1.3%, 24.0% and 74.7% of content. The 1% rule held across all four. Superusers' retention is "imperative" for long-term success. — [JMIR 2014](https://jmir.org/2014/2/e33); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24496109/)
- Claim that participation inequality is weaker in real-time chat (10–25% posting monthly) comes only from an unsourced Telegram channel citing "vendor studies"; treat as unverified opinion. — [Telegram post](https://t.me/s/ServerSignal?before=30)
- Mazzolini & Maddison (2003, _Computers & Education_): more frequent instructor posting was associated with shorter discussions and fewer student posts (secondary; original not retrieved). — [Ntourmas et al. 2018 citing it](https://www.gsic.uva.es/uploaded_files/44943_Ntourmas_etal_PCI2018_cameraReady_20181123.pdf)
- Du, Wang & Wang (HICSS 2022): in MOOCs, instructors mostly reply, but instructor posting (starting threads) was more effective than replying for student participation; the advantage grows with class size; class size weakens the benefit of replying but not of posting. — [ScholarSpace](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/7889ab2c-73fb-4a6d-a85c-3ea39fc094c9)
- Xie, Adjei & Correia (2024), _Online Learning_: compared two instructor-participation approaches; number and quality of student posts did not differ significantly. — [OLJ PDF](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/download/4573/1447/19676)
- NUS study of 61 MOOCs addresses when instructors should intervene in forum threads, given limited time. — [Chandrasekaran et al., EDM 2015](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~kanmy/papers/edm2015.pdf)
- Hew, Cheung & Ng (2010): factors behind limited contribution include students not seeing the need for discussion (relevance), instructor and peer behaviour; deadlines raise contributions. — [HKU repository](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/194283)
- WhatsApp vs LMS forum comparisons (mostly small studies, conference papers):
  - UNISA (South Africa): students prefer WhatsApp for ownership, immediacy and cost, and set up their own peer-support groups; authors see limits in WhatsApp for facilitated discussion. — [UNISA IR](https://ir.unisa.ac.za/handle/10500/23493?show=full)
  - SUTD (Singapore) chemistry course: over 83% rated WhatsApp more effective than other forums, but authors say forums are still needed for long discussion. — [SAI paper](https://ftp.saiconference.com/Downloads/Volume10No8/Paper_53-WhatsApp_as_an_Educational_Support_Tool.pdf)
  - Riyadh medical colleges (Saudi Arabia): use of the LMS and/or WhatsApp correlated with better grades; WhatsApp associated with slightly better attendance (not significant). — [PMC5768847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768847)
  - Universitas Terbuka (Indonesia): built two-way sync so WhatsApp messages appear in the LMS forum, improving tutor monitoring. — [IAFOR paper](https://papers.iafor.org/?p=64341)
  - Lombok (Indonesia), 27 students: both tools helped; recommends structured guidelines and an integrated approach. — [Unram conference paper](https://conference.unram.ac.id/event/4/contributions/105/contribution.pdf)

### Inferences

- Design responses suggested by the evidence: (a) fellows start weekly threads rather than reply to everything; (b) deadlines and clear relevance to the course; (c) identify and support "superusers" (e.g. alumni or student moderators) in programme and community groups; (d) accept that most members will read, not write, and design for readers (good summaries, searchable archive).
- The chat-app evidence suggests the in-app feature must match WhatsApp on immediacy (mobile, fast notifications) to win, while it keeps the forum's advantages (threads, records, facilitation). Students do not choose the forum only because it is better for learning.

### Gaps

- No peer-reviewed study on notification overload or tool fragmentation in course communities was found in this pass.
- Mazzolini & Maddison (2003) original was not retrieved.

## 7. Persistent, searchable archives and knowledge building (Scardamalia & Bereiter, Knowledge Forum)

### Takeaway

Knowledge-building research shows that collaborative, persistent discourse on Knowledge Forum predicts understanding beyond prior achievement, and one quasi-experiment found gains that lasted to public exams. No meta-analysis was found; evidence is mostly from school-age classrooms.

### Cited Findings

- Scardamalia (2003) overview: evaluations of CSILE/Knowledge Forum show significant gains on a wide range of cognitive indicators (citing Scardamalia, Bereiter & Lamon 1994); no effect sizes given. — [IKIT 2003 KF Advances](https://ikit.org/fulltext/2003_KFAdvances.htm); [Knowledge Forum and KB environments](https://ikit.org/fulltext/2007_KF_KBE.pdf)
- Quasi-experimental knowledge-building classroom study: students outperformed a comparison class in scientific understanding, with effects sustained into public examinations (a summary gives averages 80.6 vs 70.1; verify). Knowledge Forum collaboration and inquiry predicted understanding over and above prior science achievement. — [ijCSCL / Springer s11412-009-9069-5](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s11412-009-9069-5)
- Discourse analysis of 40 secondary students' Knowledge Forum notes distinguished knowledge sharing, knowledge construction and knowledge creation modes. — [PMC7088907](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7088907)
- Group-level formative feedback and metadiscourse study (University of Minnesota record): explores feedback to help communities reflect on their own discourse. — [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/group-level-formative-feedback-and-metadiscourse/)
- Doctoral thesis on Knowledge Building communities in higher education (University of Barcelona). — [UB thesis](https://diposit.ub.edu/items/74689ed5-2ebc-4ee1-a155-44628a8d1be4)

### Inferences

- Knowledge building theory directly matches "knowledge made together" and supports a path from discussion threads to published articles: knowledge-building principles treat ideas as improvable public objects. A WhatsApp chat is not searchable or linkable at the institution level; an in-app archive is. This is the strongest theoretical argument for owning the platform, though the empirical base for higher education is limited.

### Gaps

- No meta-analysis or pooled effect size for Knowledge Forum found.
- No study found that measures the value of searchable course-discussion archives for later cohorts.

## 8. Learning communities, cohort identity and alumni networks

### Takeaway

Learning communities raise sense of belonging in several non-randomised studies, but the largest randomised trial (MDRC, about 7,000 students) found only small, short-lived credit gains and no effect on persistence. Richer programmes (extra counselling, linked courses) did better.

### Cited Findings

- MDRC Learning Communities Demonstration: about 7,000 students randomly assigned at six community colleges, 174 learning communities, tracked three semesters. Pooled effect: about +0.5 credits in the target subject and about +0.5 total credits in the program semester; gains maintained but did not grow; no effect on persistence. — [MDRC synthesis](https://www.mdrc.org/work/projects/learning-communities-demonstration); [What have we learned](https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/what-have-we-learned-about-learning-communities-community-colleges)
- Kingsborough Opening Doors (linked three courses plus enhanced counselling and tutoring): larger effect on English credits (about one credit); six-year follow-up exists. — [MDRC Opening Doors](https://www.mdrc.org/work/projects/opening-doors?page=1)
- Two-year biology learning community at a California research university: gains in belonging, academic integration and cumulative GPA (single institution). — [PMC6430422](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6430422)
- National community-college sample of English learners (SEM): learning communities positively related to belonging, which related to persistence. — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0tb169p9)
- Small private college: learning community built class-level but not institution-level belonging. — [Bucknell](https://digitalcommons.bucknell.edu/fac_journ/1776)

### Inferences

- Course-level groups may build belonging to the cohort, but not to CILAS as a whole. Programme-wide and community-wide groups (and alumni continuity) are the logical bridge to institutional belonging; this is inference, not tested.

### Gaps

- No studies found on alumni networks in liberal-arts or small-cohort online settings, or on cohort identity carried by an online platform after a course ends.

## 9. MENA / Arab region and Arabic-language online discussion

### Takeaway

Very little research analyses Arabic-language course discussion. Available Arab-region studies use CoI with surveys and synchronous data, or compare WhatsApp with LMS.

### Cited Findings

- Lebanon (2023, _Higher Education in the Arab World_, Springer): cognitive presence in online courses at a Lebanese institution; faculty interviews, synchronous recordings and a survey of 114 students; identifies facilitators and barriers using CoI and activity theory. — [Balamand ScholarHub](https://scholarhub.balamand.edu.lb/handle/uob/7197)
- UAE–US preservice teachers: cross-cultural online discussion of multicultural literature showed social and cognitive presence linked to deep learning (AERA 2012 paper). — [AERA paper](https://convention2.allacademic.com/one/aera/aera12/online_program_direct_link/view_paper/538869/)
- Georgetown Qatar: 13 Arabic as a foreign/heritage language learners; social-presence challenges in online learning. — [UCL Discovery PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10127069/7/Maghoub_11619428116037.pdf)
- Riyadh medical colleges: LMS and/or WhatsApp use correlated with better grades. — [PMC5768847](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768847)

### Inferences

- The gap is itself relevant: a bilingual Arabic/English discussion space at CILAS would be unusual, and its archive could become a research asset.

### Gaps

- No study found on bilingual (Arabic/English) code-switching in course forums, on right-to-left forum usability, or on Egyptian higher-education forums specifically. An Arabic-language search (e.g. "منتديات النقاش التعليمية") was not run.
